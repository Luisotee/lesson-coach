# Fractions

## The idea

A fraction names equal parts of a whole. In `3/4`, the bottom number says the whole is cut into four equal parts, and the top number says we take three of them. The bottom number names the size of the parts; the top number counts them.

Equivalent fractions name the same amount with different parts. `1/2`, `2/4` and `3/6` are the same amount. Multiplying or dividing the top and the bottom by the same number keeps the amount the same.

## Why it works

You can only add things of the same kind. Halves and thirds are different sizes of part, so to add `1/2 + 1/3` we first cut both into sixths: `3/6 + 2/6`. Now the parts are the same size and we count them: five sixths, `5/6`.

Multiplying by a fraction means taking that fraction of something. `3*(2/5)` is three lots of two fifths, which is six fifths, `6/5`. Only the count of parts changes, not their size.

Dividing asks "how many of these fit into that?" `(2/3)/(3/4)` asks how many three-quarters fit into two thirds. Multiplying by the reciprocal of the divisor, `(2/3)*(4/3)`, gives `8/9`, a little less than one, which makes sense because three quarters is a little more than two thirds.

## Common misconceptions

Some students add fractions by adding the tops and adding the bottoms. They write `1/2 + 1/3` as `2/5`. But `2/5` is less than `1/2`, so adding made the amount smaller, which cannot be right.

Some students multiply both the top and the bottom by the whole number. They write `3*(2/5)` as `6/15`. That multiplies by `3/3`, which is one, so the amount has not changed at all.

Some students flip the first fraction when dividing, not the second. They write `(2/3)/(3/4)` as `(3/2)*(3/4)`, which is `9/8`. Only the divisor is turned over, because we are counting how many divisors fit.

Some students cancel across a plus sign. They divide only one term of the top by the bottom, so `(2+4)/2` becomes `2 + 2`. The fraction bar divides the whole top, so the answer is `6/2`, which is `3`.

Some students simplify by changing only one part of the fraction. They write `6/8` as `3/8`, halving the top but not the bottom. A fraction keeps its value only when the top and the bottom are divided by the same number.

## What to ask

Ask students to estimate before they calculate: is the answer more or less than one half, more or less than one? An estimate catches most of these mistakes. Ask them to draw a bar model for any fraction calculation they are unsure of.
