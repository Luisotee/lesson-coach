# How we built this, and why

A short record for reviewers. `docs/PLAN.md` has the full plan.

## The problem as we read it

Teachers who doubt their own math need two things: to feel sure of a topic, and to know what to say when a student goes wrong.

They work alone, dislike chatbots, and want nothing that grades them. With 20k–50k of them, the coach must be cheap per teacher. In a coach, a wrong math answer is the worst possible failure.

## The decisions that matter

| Decision | Why |
|---|---|
| **No chat.** Pages, buttons and structured results | The brief says teachers dislike chatbots. Buttons ("Why?", "Another way", "Picture") also keep each AI output small and checkable |
| **The LLM never computes math. sympy does** | Peer research found LLM tutors got wrong answers *and* wrong steps (32% of problems in OATutor's study). sympy computes the answers, and the LLM only explains and diagnoses with the answer given |
| **Every AI answer may say "I can't tell"** | One wrong answer is weak evidence. Forced labels made other projects invent misconceptions |
| **Known mistakes are diagnosed by rules; AI only words what to say** | If "adds tops and bottoms" reproduces the student's 2/5, the diagnosis is certain and needs no model diagnosis; one short call only words what to say for this problem (step 25) |
| **Content generated once, verified, served as static JSON** | Every teacher sees the same explanations. Generating them once makes them free per teacher and lets every one be checked before anyone sees it. No database is needed |
| **No accounts, nothing stored** | Fits "no judgment" and makes the data story short: there is no personal data to protect |
| **Math looks hand-written on squared paper** | The product owner's feedback: typed math felt like a computer, not like school |
| **Type, write or photograph the math** | Teachers write math by hand. A photo of a student's work also gives the full working, which makes the diagnosis far better. The teacher always confirms the transcription first |
| **Modern design with Akelius DNA** | Their colours, lesson conventions (balance scale, operation shown on both sides) and openness about AI, in a new and faster-feeling product |
| **Many languages, accessible from day one** | Akelius serves refugees in 13+ languages, so language and accessibility can't be added later |

## How the human and the AI worked together

I (Luis) acted as product owner. Claude Code researched, planned and designed, and Claude Code workers build. The session, step by step:

1. **Me:** "Here is the assignment; let's talk about it first." **Claude:** read the brief, the email and Akelius's site, then proposed four product ideas. Its recommendation was a lesson-prep page without chat.
2. **Me:** "I like all of them. Build the whole app with a frontend, on my Azure credits." **Claude:** checked the subscription. The allowed region `switzerlandnorth` offers `gpt-5.4-mini` on DataZoneStandard, so live AI stays in the EU data zone.
3. **Claude:** checked whether Akelius Math content could be reused. It is served by an undocumented API with no license, so we don't scrape it: we write our own lessons and deep-link to theirs.
4. **Me:** "Before building, see how similar apps are built." **Claude:** found 5 open-source peers and had an agent read each codebase against our plan. It checked their 13 findings, and these changed the plan:
   - sympy computes the answer first;
   - "I can't tell" is always an option;
   - known mistakes are matched by rules;
   - the eval set comes from a published misconception dataset.
5. **Me:** "Why JSON and not a database? Will there be a login?" **Claude:** explained why neither is needed. That led to one more change: step explanations are generated offline too, so there's no runtime cache to lose.
6. **Claude:** designed seven screens on a Claude Design canvas (desktop, tablet and phone).
7. **Me:** "Look at Akelius's own website." **Claude:** extracted their design tokens and studied a real lesson. **Me:** "Keep it modern, but it should feel like Akelius." **Claude:** rethemed the design with their colours and lesson conventions (balance scale, operation shown on both sides).
8. **Me:** "The math looks like a computer wrote it, not a person." **Claude:** switched to handwriting on squared paper, stacked fractions, steps aligned on `=`, and margin notes like `| −5`.
9. **Me:** "Can teachers write math by hand, or take a photo?" **Claude:** added type, write and photo input. The teacher confirms the transcription before anything runs, and a photo of the working shows the first line that doesn't follow.
10. **Me:** "Add every language you can, strong accessibility, a drawing board in practice, no mock buttons, and a short reasoning doc." **Claude:** wrote the final plan, a task list in waves, and agent instructions (`CLAUDE.md`).
11. **Build:** tasks are handed to Claude Code workers through agent-mesh (`/delegate-claude`). Each works on its own branch, and each PR is reviewed by another agent before merge. **T0 scaffold** set up the monorepo: FastAPI and React skeletons, the Makefile (the one place commands live), Docker, and lint and test on both sides from the first commit, so every later PR ran `make lint test`.
12. **T4 infra:** `gpt-5.4-mini` DataZoneStandard quota is 0 on Azure for Students; switched live to `gpt-5.5` DataZoneStandard to keep EU processing. Offline uses `gpt-5.5` on GlobalStandard, because GlobalBatch can't be called interactively or smoke-tested.
13. **T1 math core:** Claude wrote its own small parser instead of sympy's `parse_expr` or `parse_latex`. `parse_expr` runs Python `eval` on its input, and `parse_latex` computes as it reads (`\frac{2}{4}` becomes `1/2`, and `1\frac{1}{2}` loses its whole part). The core must keep what the student wrote, so it can tell `2/4` from `1/2` and match misconception rules. sympy still computes every value. Review found two short inputs that hung the core, so it now accepts at most 2 letters and degree 20, and the API (T6) adds a time limit. Each misconception rule now gives its own diagnosis: `wrong_inverse_order` moved to `(x + b)/a = c`, so it no longer gives the same answer as `divide_one_term`.
14. **T3 frontend:** math is drawn by our own small parser for the sympy step strings (it keeps the author's brackets and `3x` vs `3 · 5`, which a general math library would normalise away), with spoken text from locale messages under `role="math"`. The design's pencil grey (2.55:1) and the link blue on the page background (4.30:1) fail AA, so pencil is `#5b6475` and link text off white surfaces is `#2b6788`. The language picker is a native select. The language choice is the one thing kept, in the viewer's own browser (localStorage), as a convenience with no personal data; the header says so ("Only your language choice is kept, in this browser"), so "nothing stored" stays true for teacher and student data. Fonts per script load only when that language is active; CJK waits for zh-Hans in T9 (about 4.6 MB of subsets).
15. **Content (T2):** the lessons, misconceptions and hint rewrites are our own, drafted with AI and marked `ai_generated`. Practice comes from OATutor-Content and the eval seed from MaE, both at pinned commits. A pytest checks every answer with sympy, and the same check rejects an OATutor item that says `(-3)^5 = 243` (it is -243), so we left that item out. The draft pack schema lives in `backend/app/content/schema.py`, so the API and the pipeline share one set of models. The Akelius lesson links came from the site's own search box, since search engines don't index its lessons; we recorded only titles and URLs.
16. **T6 API:** a wrong answer that a sympy rule reproduces is answered from the pack's misconception card, with no LLM call; only a wrong answer no rule explains gets one LLM call, always told sympy's answer. All sympy work runs in worker processes that are killed after 3 s, because a thread can't be stopped and sympy holds the GIL. The worked-out answer on the result screen comes from a small sympy step generator, never from the LLM. A deterministic guard hides any AI text holding a number sympy didn't give it. It checks numbers only, so a wrong claim built from given numbers can still pass; the evals (T10) measure how often replies are held back as `ai_unverified_math`.
17. **T7 math input:** one `MathInput` with type, write and photo tabs gives the page the API's own shapes (`{latex}`/`{text}` or a list of `{left, right, op}` lines); written and photographed math reaches the page only after the teacher confirms each line, and an edit takes the confirmation back. MathLive (MIT) is loaded only when a field opens, so it stays out of the main bundle, and its fonts come from our build with sounds off. The drawing is sent as black ink on white without the grid, which a vision model could misread as minus signs. Photos are decoded with their EXIF rotation, cropped, turned if needed, and re-encoded as JPEG through a canvas, which drops EXIF and GPS data and keeps them under 4 MB. A line that our drawing parser can't read is still sent as written: the frontend never judges the math.
18. **T5 pipeline:** sympy writes every worked step and answer, and the LLM writes only prose: the pack text and each step's explanations. Every piece of prose must pass the sympy-based checks (claims true, a claim about the unknown true at the worked solution, numbers from its step) and a majority LLM judge, or it ships as unavailable; a part that fails a check is dropped on its own and the rest is still judged, and if the judge gives no verdict nothing ships. Misconceptions and practice are copied from the sources rather than generated. Prompts come from a fixed list per kind and language ("Work out:", "Solve for x:"), so a translation can't change their math. The English run after review made 18 generation and 22 judge calls, about $1.06, and dropped nothing. A claim about the unknown passes only as an identity or at the solution of the example it names.
19. **T8 pages:** the "Picture" explanation is drawn from the step's own math (a balance scale for an equation, a bar model for fractions, counters for small whole numbers), with the pack's `visual` sentence as its caption, so the picture always matches the verified line and needs no extra generated data. The tablet design's section tabs became an in-page section nav, so every section still prints and is found by search. The circled-mistake colour is `#b45309` (5:1), because the design's orange is 2.5:1. Packs and mistake results are mocked only in tests; the shipped UI reads the real API and shows a load error when it is down. The `/demo/math` and `/demo/input` showcases exist only in the dev build; their e2e runs against the Vite dev server.
20. **T12 deploy:** the app runs on Container Apps with 0–1 replicas: it scales to zero when idle (the first request after idle waits about 20 s), and one replica at most keeps the in-memory rate limit to one bucket per client. Azure for Students blocks ACR Tasks, so `infra/deploy.sh` builds the image with local Docker from `git archive` of the commit, which keeps `.env` and local changes out of the image. The az CLI now creates environments in an "Express" mode by default, and that mode can't pull from ACR with the app's managed identity, so the environment is pinned to WorkloadProfiles. A second client, a temporary Container Apps job, confirmed that clients get separate rate-limit buckets and that a forged `X-Forwarded-For` doesn't give a new one. `infra/smoke-app.sh` passed on the live URL, and the Playwright app specs run against it (`BASE_URL=<live-url>`) all pass: 52 on main b63d46f after T8 (the dev-only demo and input specs need the Vite dev server, so they run locally only). A Playwright global setup wakes a scaled-to-zero app first.
21. **T10 evals:** the evals drive the real API (process pool, rules, guard, Azure client) with 30 cases and 11 drawn images, 3 runs each. gpt-5.5 rejects `reasoning_effort: minimal` with a 400, so the comparison is `none` against `low`. Their accuracy was within noise (a borderline case or two flips between runs), `low` spent no reasoning tokens on diagnoses, and `none` was steadier, so `none` is now the default. The first run showed 11 of 20 nudges giving the method away, so the mistake prompt now keeps the method for the "tell"; in the final run 5 of 20 still give part of it away (mostly number-line walks). The results are hash-pinned to the prompts, packs, config defaults and the code that decides them (rules, pipeline and its math, endpoints, guard, Azure client, eval code); every output row carries that stamp, so `summarize` can't pin old outputs to new code, and judge scores and hand labels are pinned to the text they rated. Most wrong answers are answered by a rule with no LLM call, so an "AI only" mode hides the rule matches to measure the LLM's own diagnosis; its numbers are reported apart, not as product accuracy. The LLM judge's labels for calibration are Claude's, not a teacher's, and a judge dimension is quoted only when kappa ≥ 0.6.
22. **T9 languages:** the packs are translated from the committed English packs, not regenerated, so every math field and every backtick span in the prose is copied character for character and checked (`translation_findings`); the only allowed difference is a bare number up to 10 said as a word, which Arabic needs for its dual ("two parts" is one word). Prose digits stay 0-9 in every language, like the math. The first French run cost $5.39: the judge flagged style preferences and the loop resampled whole units, which never converged. The judge now reports only errors (meaning, math terms, grammar), and only the failing texts are asked for again, which brought a language to about $1.2 for packs and $0.55 for UI strings. UI strings must compile as ICU for their locale with the same arguments and the locale's plural categories, checked with formatjs's own parser. Review found that en.json used the CLDR `one` category to mean the number 1, so French, Portuguese, Hindi, Bengali and Persian read 0/3 as "one third" and Russian read 21/4 as "one quarter"; en.json now says a literal one as `=1`, and a check (pipeline and vitest) makes every plural say its count where the English does. The Russian fraction and an Arabic dual were then fixed by hand and passed the judge; the judge also once flagged Arabic's singular noun after 100 ("مئة سطر"), which is correct Arabic, and passed it on the next review. The lead raised the cap to $53 for these fixes. 18 languages ship complete for $52.60 (en plus ar, fr, de, el, it, pt, pt-BR, ru, es, sv, pl, kk, tr, fa, ur, hi, bn, zh-Hans). uk and sw failed their spoken fractions three times, so their packs were removed too: the API serves only languages the app offers. so, ti, ps and prs were not reached. Some early runs' call counts were not recorded before stats were added up across runs (fr, ar, zh-Hans, kk, sw packs; ru, sv, pl, ar UI), so their counts in the report are low; costs were always recorded. Every Chinese code (zh-TW, zh-Hant too) gets Simplified Chinese, the one Chinese we have, since it is closer than English for most readers. Every language but English is marked machine-translated (evals/translation-report.md).
23. **T11 accessibility:** every page state is checked with axe, for reflow and for clipped text at five layouts; 200% zoom is tested as a 640×400 CSS viewport (what a 1280×800 window shows at 200%), which found focused elements hidden under the sticky header, now fixed with `scroll-padding-top` from the header's live height. Focus rings are navy everywhere: the shadcn default (link blue at 50 % alpha) was about 2:1, under 1.4.11, and the keyboard test now checks the ring's width and contrast, not just that one exists. In the other languages, the spoken-math check formats the expected label from each locale's own ICU templates, and the font check asks Chromium which fonts drew the text (CDP `CSS.getPlatformFontsForNode`): the computed `font-family` is the same stack in every language and proves nothing. No human has used it with a real screen reader yet; `docs/A11Y.md` says so and gives the script for that pass.

24. **T13 delivery:** `README.md` answers the brief item by item and links to where each is built; [`DATA.md`](../DATA.md) says in plain words what data goes where; [`docs/COST.md`](COST.md) prices the measured tokens at the Azure retail prices. Writing DATA.md turned up two things to state rather than gloss over: Microsoft's abuse monitoring may keep flagged prompts in the resource's geography (we have not applied to turn it off), and the photo screen doesn't yet ask teachers to keep names and faces out of the picture. `make zip` builds the delivery zip from a fresh clone, so it carries `.git` and nothing local. The four questions drafted for Mykola (`docs/PLAN.md`) were not sent; we built on stated assumptions instead (listed there).
25. **Rule matches, after the live test:** on `3x + 5 = 20` → `25/3`, the rule was right but "what you could say" was the card's moves for its example `2*x + 6 = 10` ("get rid of the +6"), shown as if about the student's problem: unverified math. A rule match now makes one short structured call that words the card's moves for this problem, given sympy's findings and the card as a style example, and checked by the numbers guard against the student's math only, so the example's numbers fail it. The card's own moves are used as they are only for its own example and wrong answer (compared by value, so `x = 8` is `8`); the response says who wrote the moves (`say_source`), and only AI-worded moves carry the AI note. If the call is unavailable, abstains, leaves a move blank or fails the guard, the card's moves are shown under "Written for an example problem, not your student's:" and that problem, or, on the card's own problem with another wrong answer, under "Written for another wrong answer to this problem:" and that answer; the call has its own 6 s deadline (it takes about 2 s), so a slow model doesn't hold back a diagnosis sympy settled. Worded moves are marked "worded by AI for this problem · numbers checked by the math engine", since the rule badge says nothing about AI. This costs a call per rule match ($0.0074), which moves AI per teacher from $0.07 to $0.10 a month; skipping it would mean always showing the framed example, which a teacher would have to translate to their problem. The same review dropped the rule path's explanation (it repeated the belief and why it feels right) and stopped offering, as "try next", the problem just typed: a practice problem of the topic comes before another card's check question, which was built to detect another belief. Problems typed without a kind are read as `evaluate`, so `evaluate` and `simplify` compare as one. In the eval rerun all 78 rule matches got moves written for their problem and none fell back; the judge (uncalibrated) rated 15 of 78 of their nudges at least partly revealing, against 20 of 144 for the AI's own diagnoses.

Every factual claim (licenses, dataset contents, model availability) was checked before it went into the plan.

## Time

The brief asks for two hours. We chose to deliver a complete, working product instead, and report the real time here. Human time and agent time are counted separately.

| Phase | Wall clock (2026-11-18, UTC−3) | Luis, active | Claude, active |
|---|---|---|---|
| Understanding, research, plan, peer review, design | 01:50–05:48 (4 h; peer agents researched for 2 h of it) | ~10 min, 25 prompts | ~76 min |
| Build, T0–T13 (agent-mesh run `m956c`) | 05:47–12:32+ (6.75 h, of which 2.5 h idle: no agent ran from 08:03 to 10:32) | a few short messages, no task or code input | ~14.75 h summed over parallel agents: workers ~8.4 h, reviewers ~3.4 h, lead ~3 h. Some agent was working during ~4.3 h of wall clock |

**How we measured.**
- **Wall clock:** the first and last timestamps of the planning session and of the mesh log.
- **Claude's active time:** each Claude Code transcript's timestamps, counting the gap between two consecutive events only when it is 5 minutes or less, so waiting and idle time are left out. Agents running at the same time are summed.
- **Luis's time:** the 25 prompts in the planning transcript, and his own estimate of about 10 minutes. During the build he sent the lead a handful of short messages (asking whether it was finished; deciding not to send the questions for Mykola), with no task or code input. Every task, review and merge was sent by the lead agent.
- **The git history starts with the plan**, at 05:39, after the planning. Per-task commit times are not a good measure, because rebases rewrite them; the mesh log has each task's send and done times.
- **Not measured:** time Luis spent reading or testing the app outside these sessions.

## Known limits and next steps

- **Accessibility:** no human has done a screen-reader pass yet (NVDA or VoiceOver), and no native speaker has checked any translation ([`docs/A11Y.md`](A11Y.md)).
- **Handwriting transcription** was evaluated only on synthetic drawn images (45 of 45 lines right). Real teacher photos of student work are needed before trusting it.
- **The LLM judges aren't calibrated against teachers.** The diagnosis judge's hand labels are Claude's, and no judge dimension reached kappa ≥ 0.6, so no judge score is quoted as quality. The translation judge has no human labels at all.
- **Languages:** uk and sw were dropped (their spoken fractions failed three times); so, ti, ps and prs were not reached.
- **Model:** `gpt-5.4-mini` has 0 Data Zone quota on this subscription, so live calls use the costlier `gpt-5.5`. Swapping is config only, but the evals must be re-run first.
- **Scale:** the in-memory rate limit holds the app to one replica. 20,000+ teachers need a shared rate limit first ([`docs/COST.md`](COST.md)).
- **Diagnosis:** when no rule explains a wrong answer, the model names the right misconception in about half the cases (4 of 8). More rules raise both accuracy and savings.
